# Human Natural Translation

Preserve what the writing does to and between people, along with what it says. A target-language reader should be able to recognize the situation, make the intended inference, and experience a comparable feeling without mentally translating back into the source language. Literal wording is a means, not the measure of fidelity. Aim for comparable understanding and emotional effect; do not promise an identical response from every reader.

Human Natural Translation is the shared approach. Poetic Natural is its poetry mode. Natural does not mean simpler, smoother, more uplifting, or more ornate. Preserve a writer's awkwardness when it carries intention; remove awkwardness introduced by translation.

## Human effect and cultural understanding

Read for the human event: who is speaking to whom, what each person knows, what is left unsaid, and what the reader is meant to notice or feel. Identify whether the passage teases, accuses, comforts, embarrasses, praises ironically, withholds an answer, or changes emotional direction. A proverb's moral may survive while its joke or social use disappears; that is a meaningful loss, not a successful equivalent.

Choose phrasing for the actual target audience and context. Cultures are not uniform: audience familiarity, period, relationship, formality and genre matter. Use context when it is clear; ask only when an audience choice would materially change the result. Do not assume that every English reader is American or that shared vocabulary makes an Odia expression work automatically in Hindi or Tamil.

Let idiom, syntax, dialogue timing, register and line breaks change when needed to carry the same implication and emotional force. A familiar target-language expression is useful only if its tone, intensity, social relationship and inference fit; matching the dictionary meaning is insufficient. Preserve meaningful strangeness while removing avoidable barriers to understanding. Do not explain a punchline or replace an emotional moment with its moral.

Distinguish flexible expression from cultural anchors. Ordinary idiomatic wording can be recast freely within its meaning. Names, settings, objects, myths and social roles that carry the work's identity need to remain recognizable. Replacing a prayer room and bananas with a kitchen and cookies may serve a requested everyday cultural adaptation; it changes the scene and is not an automatic choice for a poem. Identify a material cultural substitution as adaptation in the working explanation or private editorial record; do not add unrequested public attribution labels. Read [cultural effect and choice](references/cultural-effect.md) when literal fidelity and audience recognition pull apart.

## Source and meaning

Read the complete poem or prose section before translating. Use the latest approved source, not an old recording transcript, a previous translation, or an illustration narrative. Record section identity and a source fingerprint in the project's review records. Load supplied glossaries, author explanations, and approved corrections first. An unfamiliar dialect word is not automatically an error. Never silently normalize it or replace a confirmed meaning with a plausible dictionary guess.

Identify who speaks, who acts, who receives the action, what changes, and where the emotional or rhetorical turn occurs. Preserve negation, degree, tense, repetitions, contrasts, and uncertain agency. Distinguish author-confirmed meaning from your interpretation. When uncertainty materially changes the passage, keep a provisional draft, explain the exact uncertainty privately, and continue independent sections. Do not invent an answer or block a whole collection over one word.

Translate directly from the source where capable. A bridge translation is a working aid, not the new authority; disclose its use in the private record and check decisive choices against the source. Translate each target language independently so one English interpretation does not silently determine all versions.

## Choose the mode

**Human Natural** applies directly to dialogue, sayings and other expressions: preserve the recognizable human behavior, tone and social effect in language someone would plausibly use in that situation. A short answer need not expose the full translation process.

**Poetic Natural** is the default for poems. Carry the shared Human Natural approach into a complete poetic experience: voice, images, emotional distance, political bite, tenderness, irony, silence, ambiguity and the final resonance. Ask where the reader smiles, recoils, hesitates or makes a discovery, and whether the target poem allows a comparable experience. Preserve the timing of revelation; explaining an image too early can undo the poem.

Compose cadence through the target language's breath, syntax, sound and recurrence. Recast phrasing and relineate when literal structure weakens meaning or feeling; preserve meaningful stanza and emotional turns without requiring identical syllables, line lengths or surface wording. Do not force rhyme or a fixed meter unless requested. Do not add decorative similes, morals, universal lessons or explanations of symbolism. Do not turn a short poem into explanatory prose.

**Reflective Prose** applies to introductions, essays, and prose passages. Preserve argument, qualification, rhetorical questions, and the author's pace without copying source-language syntax mechanically. Translate quoted verse as verse when its structure is established. Do not abridge a long introduction without instruction.

Keep proper names and culturally meaningful references identifiable. Choose retention, a natural equivalent, transliteration or a light contextual phrase according to the passage and its effect. When a substitution would relocate the scene or alter its identity, treat it as adaptation rather than quietly importing it into a faithful book translation. Keep extended explanations outside the literary text. Consult [language notes](references/languages.md) for English, Tamil, and Hindi.

## Draft and review

1. Draft the complete unit in the target language, then read it for a coherent speaking voice. Preserve its real title; an opening-line title follows the translated opening, while a numbered poem retains its number. Never invent titles to make the book sound literary.
2. Compare source and target passage by passage. Check omissions/additions, image, agency, negation, quantities, power relations, cultural roles, satire, and the final turn. A literal back-translation is a diagnostic aid, not proof of fidelity.
3. Compare the human effect: does the reader infer the same unstated point, encounter the emotional turn at the right moment, and feel a comparable degree of humor, tenderness, discomfort or sting? Check that natural wording has not made the speaker harsher, more reverent, more sentimental or more certain. If literal and idiomatic candidates differ materially, compare them privately and record the tradeoff.
4. Read the target aloud internally for unnatural calques, inflated diction, register drift, repetitive sentence patterns, forced solemnity, and gratuitous rhyme. Preserve deliberate repetition instead of varying synonyms for elegance.
5. Record unresolved decisions and actual review evidence outside the poem. AI self-review, fluent-reader review, and author approval are different events. Never imply a native speaker, independent reviewer, or poet has reviewed text unless that happened. A source-word explanation is not approval of a whole translation.

Use the [review record](references/review-record.md) when storing a book translation or substantial batch. For a short in-chat translation, provide the requested text with only material uncertainties; do not impose a form or publication ceremony.

## Book workflow

Begin with a small representative pilot when a voice has not yet been agreed, then work in bounded section/language batches. Preserve previous drafts and a shared, contextual glossary. Choose batch size by the length and difficulty of the passages. Keep progress explicit: translated, self-reviewed, awaiting fluent review, ready under the project's release rule, or published. Completion is not the same as release readiness.

Respect existing draft/ready and publication rules. Do not publish or overwrite approved versions merely because a skill was invoked. Use existing authorization without asking again. Verify section coverage, title/stanza order, Unicode, script-specific font support, pagination, and language-specific bookmarks. A changed source invalidates the old source review; flag affected drafts for comparison rather than silently claiming they remain current.
