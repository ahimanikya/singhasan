Pravakar Satapathy’s Singhasan:
A Manifesto for a Free Life

Reading Pravakar Satapathy’s Singhasan (2001, 2017), one encounters a fierce compassion. This is not the compassion of despair; nor the helplessness of people caught in the tentacular grip of an ailing social life and a politics stripped of values; nor the fear-ridden despondency brought on by the tyranny of ruthless power. Rather, it is a compassion grounded in solidarity, fellow feeling, and the desire for change. It urges us to keep going, to keep the search alive. It seeks to break the chains of servitude and give new form to the bonds that hold a common life together. In the poet’s words, “For me, poetry bears freedom and liberation.” This compassion does not chant a mantra of destruction. It offers words to bring about a change of heart.

Singhasan contains sixty-eight poems. Their poetic vision examines power, its distribution, and its effects through subtle symbolism. Mythology, history, legend, contemporary political arrangements, and folktales sharpen that vision and deepen the poetry. Behind it lies a dream that honours human dignity; an unyielding strength to make a new world through goodwill; a surge of self-belief suffused with sweetness. The poet first attempted to write in the nine-syllable metre of the Bhagavata, but later, for various reasons, left that form and embraced free verse. In these poems, readers can hear the music of liberation and encounter their own desire to rise beyond themselves. They can free themselves from the ravenous impulses that power feeds.

The poet’s intention finds expression in these lines:

Peace, friendship, love will come;
there will be no fear of the throne.
The weight of sorrow will grow light;
the earth will leap with joy.
No one will stand as ruler;
no people will be ruled.
The swagger of the lion-seat
will no longer lord it over the earth.
A new Purana will be written;
old ways of thinking will break apart.
A free life does not want
another to become its master.
A common mantra will be spoken;
equality will prevail throughout the land.
There will be no hatred or discrimination,
no hunger and no want.
The earth will smile at sunrise;
words will come forth without fear.
Everyone will become a poet;
the world will shine.

(“Everyone Will Become a Poet”)

The poetic vision expressed in this Bhagavata metre has two aspects:

(1) The throne creates an order founded on fear. In the prevailing social system, what ought to be the basis of order has become the chains of a prison. If we are to achieve the equality necessary for solidarity, society faces the challenge of finding an alternative to the throne.

(2) The poet feels the need for a poetic spirit if the fundamental elements of solidarity—peace, friendship, love, equality, and modern critical thought—are to develop. Hence his insistence that “everyone will become a poet.” What does the poetic spirit possess that could offer an alternative to power centred on the throne?

When Plato advises Glaucon to banish poetry from his Republic, or proposes admitting only a particular kind of poetry that praises God or good people, Pravakar’s faith in poets opens up a thought-provoking debate. It seems useful here to set out the Republic’s views on poetry and the arguments behind them.

(a) Poetry, particularly dramatic poetry, has a formidable capacity to present audiences with distorted representations of good character. These may undermine the rational principles of the state. It must therefore be excluded.

(b) Art or poetry indulges the lower appetites of human nature, far removed from considerations of truth or justice. In consequence, it obstructs the proper conduct of the state. It is therefore unacceptable.

(c) Much emotionally stirring poetry misrepresents gods or heroes and may thereby encourage moral decline. God is the cause only of good; therefore, Plato holds, God must never be misrepresented: “God is perfectly good and therefore changeless and incapable of deceit, and must never be otherwise represented” (The Republic, p. 129). When a poet writes, for example, “Zeus has two Jars standing on the floor of his palace, full of fates, good in one and evil in the other,” this is a false representation of God. The Greek word for good, agathos, has a particular meaning: morally good and conducive to the welfare of people. God is therefore the cause only of good: “God is the cause, not of all things, but only of good.” If poetry presents an idea that diminishes God’s dignity, it will harm the state Plato envisages. Such poetry cannot be accepted.

Let us now turn to Pravakar’s position. Why does a healthy society and state need the poetic spirit? Because of its inward insight. From that insight comes the belief that a way can be found to save social solidarity and organisation from destruction. A questioning outlook gives us the impetus to confront our circumstances. Inward insight gives coherence to consciousness. In his discussion of “The ideal spirit of poetry,” Sri Aurobindo asks: in an age when the mind grows ever more searching, what does the ideal power of poetry do? He answers: “Within us, insight takes up the ‘ego’ and reality and assumes the form of God as a liberated soul;[1] it takes the form of the God of truth, freedom, and unity; of the God of visionary will and power; of the God of love and universal delight; of the God of universal and eternal beauty; of the God of supreme light, supreme harmony, and supreme good.” Poetry endowed with insight awakens five enduring powers: truth, beauty, joy, life, and self-awareness. Put a little differently, the inward insight of the poetic spirit opens a creative path towards reassessing reality. Whether or not this vision takes material form, it draws within the heart an image of self-governance.

In Will to power 2: The will to the End, the philosopher Nietzsche offers a significant proposition: to give birth to a dancing star, one must harbour a terrible turmoil within. “One must have chaos inside in order to give birth to a dancing star” (p. 1). Reading Singhasan, the reader experiences the birth of a star out of chaos. Let us look at the first poem:

A quarrel began.
Neither women nor gold were at stake.
The quarrel grew.
Anxiety took hold: would disaster follow?

…
A sturdy young man came running,
like a morning bathed in gold.
From a high mound of earth,
he gave orders with the bearing of a king.
…
The uproar died in a moment.
As they dug into the earth, they discovered
a throne hidden beneath the high mound.

This throne is a symbol of order. It serves the people’s welfare and gives substance to the idea of the state. It establishes Vikramaditya’s good governance and points towards a golden age. As the guardian of justice and ethics, it guides the public conscience in distinguishing good from evil. Look a little deeper, and the dramatic emergence of the throne in this poem reveals the contours of Plato’s ideal state.

In most of the poems that follow, however, the throne—whether of monarchy or democracy—gives rise to catastrophe, destruction, and anarchy. Behind its attractive exterior lie immorality, inhumanity, and the unequal dispensation of justice. They have made the throne bloodthirsty and consumed by lust. In the poet’s words:

“Nothing else is true
in this royal game.
Only fear and arrogance are true.
… The golden crown of ideals
is sometimes a reason for patricide,
sometimes the finest turn of phrase
for cutting off a golden nose.” (p. 23)

In most of the collection, the poet gives poignant expression to moral decay, the deceit of the hunger for power, the desperate competition for wealth, and the inequalities and exclusions people themselves create. Folktales, myths, figures from the Puranas, and scriptural sayings give depth to his account of these distortions and to the feelings it communicates. Wordplay and subtle satire give the poems a sharp vision and a power that sears the heart. Their intellectual force also offers readers a chance to see anew. Krishna’s arrival at Kamsa’s arena, for instance, promises the people protection from anarchy. The reader can understand how the following comes crashing down:

“In the voices of the Chanuras,
the challenge to a game
that mixes poison with trust;
the stages taking shape out of sight.” (p. 19)

The Odia saying “A clueless king and an inept minister” points to a habit of unthinking judgement embedded in the popular mind.

“Whether he understands nothing
or keeps inept ministers,
whether he builds splendid palaces
or makes rivers run
with the blood of countless subjects,
it makes no difference.
It is all the throne’s doing.” (p. 34)

Here the ruler’s personality is secondary. What matters is the supremacy of the throne.

Through the horrors of the Kalinga War, the history of a great people offered the world a different narrative. The poet gives conscious expression to this in his writing. The poem begins:

“There was no water in the riverbed;
there was blood, there was blood.”

And it ends with “an image of compassion—the extraordinary work of a heart transformed.” In a few words, the poem shows how effectively myth can bring history home to the reader.

Plato describes four corrupted forms of government arising from the misuse of power: timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and tyranny. In timocracy, decline begins in social conflict. The individual within this system is hopeful and sportsmanlike, yet inwardly beset by uncertainty:

“All manner of instruments are playing;
the capital looks like a queen.
… Yes, this is the same man
who once cried,
‘Give us freedom; take our blood.’
Today he advances
with the bearing of a king.” (p. 46)

Who can say whether that king will remain? Who can tell when the sceptre of justice will become a weapon tucked into someone’s waistband? As the poet puts it:

“The weapon does not pierce the waist;
it pierces the conscience.
It places a crown upon a man without courage.”

Look, reader, at the precarious state of government.

The oligarchic ruler’s chief attraction is wealth. His character is that of a male bee—a drone. To live in luxury without working is his abuse of power. Corruption, the conversion of national wealth into private property, and patronage define his rule. When a leader of democracy is led through the streets in handcuffs, he tells the people in his defence:

“I have committed no great sin.
I thought the nation’s wealth was mine
and merely stored it away
in a granary that stood empty.” (p. 89)

At such moments, the workings of oligarchic rule become plain to the reader:

“Everyone’s mouth is shut
with a handful of puffed rice.
The bargain is sealed
in a night as dark as kohl.
… Let blood flow, let fires burn,
let the cannons roar—
none of it matters.” (p. 49)

Who among us has not encountered this headless body of oligarchy in our own lives?

Democracy is another distortion. The dream of democracy rests on the two principles of equality and individual freedom. Francis Fukuyama takes liberal democracy as his measure of human progress; in Plato’s scheme of government, it is a corruption. Plato points to the gulf between the ideal and the real. “Attachment to wealth and the citizen’s self-restraint cannot go together,” he says (The Republic, p. 373). He calls it “an agreeable anarchic form of society” (p. 376). Democracy contains diversity alongside a condition without ethical restraint. Singhasan offers many instances:

“The streets are awash with blood;
the horizon glows red.
Take a note, cast a vote—
the bugle of democracy sounds.” (p. 48)

Another poem presents a further, pitiful scene of this same condition:

“The child watching the play
cannot understand:
the king of democracy
in a government emptied of rule,
in misrule with no governance.
… What country is this,
and who, then, is that king?
What fear for his throne
makes him tremble
and set others trembling?” (p. 92)

This ungoverned misrule is as heavy a burden to bear as it is difficult to comprehend.

Now, tyranny. It is born of the conflict between rich and poor within democracy. In Plato’s words, “Tyrant is a parricide” (p. 391). He is not merely a killer of his own kin; he is a parasitic drone who exploits others. The people he exploits are unresisting, easy prey. Many strange things happen here. Pravakar presents one such development strikingly:

“The throne is no longer something to ascend;
in history,
it has become something to seize.” (p. 41)

Elsewhere, a citizen says:

“I have sold myself entire.
Where is there still a voice
that will shatter
the autocrat’s terrible throne?” (p. 99)

Is there really no voice left? Let us return to that fierce compassion. The poet is afflicted by the wretchedness of the situation, certainly, but he is not subdued. The fierce surge of compassion within him is ready to strike at this catastrophe. The throne’s bloody form leaves him neither resigned nor cowed. Among those people who have sold themselves, a naked child can still say to the king:

“King, why aren’t you wearing any clothes?” (p. 94)

That naked child is none other than the poetic vision of fierce compassion. Within the poet is a visionary will capable of dreaming a different world into being. In this alternative world, “the throne fades away into a meaningless, useless word.” From the lilting sound of a kadamba branch, another voice rises to awaken the dead. This is not the voice of a system of power, but the power of love. Another road opens, along which humanity may be enthroned in the heart. It is the road to a free life, not to unchecked self-will. On this road there is no hatred, no hunger, no oppression, no swagger of the lion-seat. Here a new life has its sunrise; here we hear the song of the fearless human spirit awakening to itself. Would it be wrong to call this a gateway to freedom and liberation?
